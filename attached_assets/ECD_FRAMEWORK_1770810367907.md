# Evidence-Centered Design (ECD) Framework
## Assessment Architecture for Project Euclid Curriculum

**Version:** 2.0 (February 2026)  
**Purpose:** Define assessment structure ensuring geometric learning is observable, measurable, and meaningful

---

## Core Principle

Evidence-Centered Design provides the **temporal anchor** for the Project Euclid curriculum. Rather than forcing historical content onto an external timeline ("In 3500 BCE..."), ECD allows us to define moments in time through **graduations of pomp and circumstance** - the observable demonstrations of student understanding.

**The breakthrough insight:** We're not writing a history curriculum that happens to use geometry. We're writing a **geometry and art curriculum** that uses historical civilizations as context. ECD frees us to organize time according to **learning moments** rather than chronological accuracy.

---

## What ECD Solves: The Timeline Crisis

### The Problem
Traditional curriculum design creates false dependencies:
- "Students must learn about Sumer before Babylon because Babylon came later"
- "We can't teach Greek geometry until we've covered Egypt"
- Forces linear progression through time even when concepts don't require it

### The Solution
ECD reframes time as **assessment moments:**
- Week 1: Student demonstrates understanding of circles through Mesopotamian context
- Week 2: Student demonstrates understanding of stars through same civilization
- Week 8: Student demonstrates understanding of hexagons, still in Mesopotamia

**The order is pedagogical, not chronological.** We sequence by geometric concept difficulty and developmental appropriateness, not by when things happened in history.

### Why This Works
We're not teaching "ancient history" - we're teaching geometry. The historical context serves the geometric learning, not vice versa. Students don't need to know that the 8-pointed star came chronologically after the circle in Mesopotamian art; they need to master circle properties before tackling rotational symmetry.

**ECD gives us permission to organize time around learning, not calendars.**

## Three-Layer Assessment Architecture

### Layer 1: Claims (What We Assert Students Know)
**Definition:** Statements about student knowledge, skills, or understanding that the curriculum seeks to develop

**Types of claims:**

#### Knowledge Claims
"Student knows that..."
- A circle is defined as all points equidistant from a center
- Shamash was the Mesopotamian god of sun and justice
- The potter's wheel was invented around 3500 BCE

#### Skill Claims
"Student can..."
- Identify circles in ancient artifacts
- Calculate the circumference using ancient approximations
- Create a cylinder seal design using circular motifs

#### Understanding Claims
"Student understands that..."
- Geometric forms carry both mathematical and cultural meaning
- The same shape (circle) can have different symbolic significance across cultures
- Mathematical properties enable functional inventions (rotational symmetry → potter's wheel)

### Layer 2: Evidence (What We Observe)
**Definition:** Observable student actions, products, or performances that indicate whether claims are valid

**Evidence types:**

#### Written Responses
- Short answer questions
- Diagram labeling
- Explanation paragraphs
- Exit tickets

#### Physical Products
- Drawn geometric designs
- Constructed models
- Clay seal impressions
- Painted artifacts

#### Performance Tasks
- Oral presentations
- Demonstrations of technique
- Collaborative projects
- Role-play scenarios

#### Process Observations
- Work samples showing reasoning
- Peer discussion participation
- Problem-solving approaches
- Self-reflection journals

### Layer 3: Tasks (What We Ask Students to Do)
**Definition:** Specific activities designed to elicit the evidence needed to support claims

**Task design principles:**

#### Authentic Context
Tasks should feel meaningful, not arbitrary:
- "Design a cylinder seal for Shamash" > "Draw 5 circles"
- "Explain why ancient potters needed math" > "Define rotational symmetry"

#### Multiple Access Points
Tasks accommodate different strengths:
- Visual learners: Draw and label
- Verbal learners: Explain orally
- Kinesthetic learners: Build a model
- Logical learners: Solve a calculation

#### Graduated Challenge
Tasks include scaffolding for struggling students and extensions for advanced:
- Basic: Identify the circular elements in this artifact
- Proficient: Explain how the circular design relates to Shamash mythology
- Advanced: Compare circular symbolism across three civilizations

## ECD Application to Mesopotamia Pilot

### Week 1: Circle (Shamash)

**Claim 1 (Knowledge):** Student knows that a circle is the set of all points equidistant from a center

**Evidence:**
- Can identify radius, diameter, center on diagrams
- Can explain in own words what makes a circle different from oval
- Can locate circular elements in Mesopotamian artifacts

**Task:** Examine the Shamash Tablet relief. Label the circular sun disk with: center, radius (at least 3 rays), diameter (across full disk). Write 2 sentences explaining what makes this shape a perfect circle.

---

**Claim 2 (Understanding):** Student understands that circles held both mathematical and divine significance in Mesopotamia

**Evidence:**
- Can articulate Shamash's connection to circular imagery
- Can explain at least one reason circles were considered special
- Can identify circles in both religious and functional contexts

**Task:** Read the myth excerpt about Shamash's daily journey. Then examine 3 artifacts:
1. Shamash Tablet (religious)
2. Clay pot rim (functional)
3. Cylinder seal (administrative)

Write a paragraph explaining: Why did Mesopotamians use circles in SO many different ways? What did circles mean to them beyond just math?

---

**Claim 3 (Skill):** Student can apply understanding of rotational symmetry to create original designs

**Evidence:**
- Creates design incorporating circular/radial elements
- Demonstrates understanding of symmetry through intentional pattern choices
- Can explain geometric decisions made during creation

**Task:** You are a Mesopotamian seal-carver creating a new seal for a priest of Shamash. Requirements:
- Must include circular sun disk
- Must have at least 6 rays showing rotational symmetry
- Must include one other circular element (rosette, eye, etc.)
- Draw your design on paper, then carve it into clay
- Write 3 sentences explaining your geometric choices

---

### Week 2: 8-Pointed Star (Ishtar)

**Claim 1 (Knowledge):** Student knows that an 8-pointed star is formed by rotating a square 45° and overlapping

**Evidence:**
- Can construct 8-pointed star using geometric method
- Can identify 8-fold rotational symmetry
- Can measure 45° angles between points

**Task:** Using a square template and protractor, construct a perfect 8-pointed star. Label:
- The two overlapping squares
- The 45° rotation angle
- All 8 points

Then find this star pattern in 3 different Mesopotamian artifacts (use image library).

---

**Claim 2 (Understanding):** Student understands the connection between Ishtar's mythology and the 8-pointed star symbol

**Evidence:**
- Can explain Ishtar's domains (love, war, Venus)
- Can articulate why 8-pointed star was chosen (Venus cycle)
- Can describe how geometry reinforces mythological meaning

**Task:** Research prompt: "Ishtar was associated with the planet Venus. Observe Venus in the sky for 8 years, and you'll notice it makes a near-perfect 8-pointed star pattern as it moves."

Question: Why is the 8-pointed star a PERFECT symbol for Ishtar? Write a paragraph explaining how this geometric shape connects to:
1. Her association with Venus
2. The mathematical pattern Venus makes
3. Why this made the star feel "special" or "magical" to Mesopotamians

---

**Claim 3 (Skill):** Student can create tessellation patterns using 8-pointed stars

**Evidence:**
- Demonstrates understanding of tessellation (covering plane without gaps)
- Uses 8-pointed stars as repeating unit
- Maintains symmetry across pattern

**Task:** Create a tessellation pattern for the walls of Ishtar's temple using 8-pointed stars. Your pattern must:
- Cover the entire space (no large gaps)
- Use at least 12 stars
- Show color/decoration choices
- Include at least one other geometric shape to fill gaps (squares, diamonds, triangles)

Reflection question: Why is tessellation important for architecture? How did ancient builders use repeated patterns?

## Assessment Rubric Structure

### Four Performance Levels

**Level 1: Emerging (Below Grade Level)**
- Partial understanding of geometric concepts
- Requires significant support to complete tasks
- Evidence shows gaps in foundational knowledge
- Limited connection between math and cultural context

**Level 2: Developing (Approaching Grade Level)**
- Demonstrates basic understanding of geometric properties
- Completes tasks with some support
- Makes simple connections between math and culture
- Shows accuracy in straightforward applications

**Level 3: Proficient (At Grade Level)**
- Clear understanding of geometric concepts and properties
- Completes tasks independently with accuracy
- Articulates connections between GEK and GEpHR
- Applies geometric thinking to novel situations

**Level 4: Advanced (Above Grade Level)**
- Deep, nuanced understanding of geometric relationships
- Completes tasks with sophistication and creativity
- Makes unexpected connections across concepts
- Demonstrates transfer to other contexts/civilizations

### Rubric Example: Circle Construction Task

| Criterion | Emerging (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|----------------|--------------|
| **Geometric Accuracy** | Circle is irregular; center is not marked; radius is inconsistent | Circle is mostly round; center is marked; some radii are unequal | Circle is accurate; center is precise; all radii are equal within tolerance | Circle is perfect; construction method shows understanding of tools (compass, string); multiple circles show consistent technique |
| **Labeling** | Missing 2+ required labels; labels are incorrect or unclear | Missing 1 label OR labels lack precision | All required labels present and accurate | Labels include additional geometric insights (chord, arc, sector) not required |
| **Mathematical Explanation** | Cannot explain what makes a circle | Partial explanation (e.g., "round shape") | Clear explanation using correct vocabulary (equidistant, center, radius) | Explanation includes comparative analysis (circle vs ellipse) or historical method (ancient compass techniques) |
| **Cultural Connection** | No connection made to Mesopotamian context | Identifies that circles appear in artifacts but cannot explain why | Explains Shamash connection and at least one reason circles mattered | Compares Mesopotamian circular symbolism to another civilization; shows understanding of universal vs culturally-specific meanings |

## GEpHR and GEK Integration

### Two Complementary Assessment Streams

**Day A Assessments (GEpHR - Geometric Element as Metaphor)**
Focus on cultural, symbolic, aesthetic dimensions

**Evidence types:**
- Written explanations of mythological connections
- Artistic recreations showing cultural patterns
- Comparative analysis across civilizations
- Reflection on symbolic meaning

**Example task:** "Create an artifact in Mesopotamian style that shows how circles represented divine order and justice through Shamash imagery"

---

**Day B Assessments (GEK - Geometric Element as Knowledge)**
Focus on mathematical properties, scientific applications, functional uses

**Evidence types:**
- Calculations and measurements
- Construction demonstrations
- Problem-solving with geometric principles
- Technical explanations of mechanisms

**Example task:** "Calculate the circumference of a Mesopotamian clay storage vessel using the ancient approximation π ≈ 3. Explain how potters would have used this calculation."

---

**Bridge Assessments (Both)**
Integrate symbolic and functional understanding

**Evidence types:**
- Projects showing both artistic and mathematical sophistication
- Explanations connecting form and function
- Innovations that required both streams of knowledge

**Example task:** "Design a cylinder seal that tells a story about Shamash (GEpHR) while demonstrating understanding of how rotational motion transforms 3D carving into 2D print (GEK)"

## Formative vs Summative Assessment

### Formative Assessment (During Learning)
**Purpose:** Provide feedback to guide instruction and student progress

**Frequency:** Daily/weekly throughout each element study

**Methods:**
- Exit tickets (1-2 questions checking understanding)
- Observation during hands-on activities
- Quick sketches showing geometric understanding
- Think-pair-share discussions
- Self-assessment checklists

**Key principle:** Formative assessment is LOW STAKES. Students should feel safe to make mistakes and reveal misconceptions so teachers can adjust instruction.

### Summative Assessment (After Learning)
**Purpose:** Evaluate mastery of claims for that geometric element

**Frequency:** End of each week (per element)

**Methods:**
- Complete project demonstrating GEpHR + GEK
- Written test covering key concepts
- Performance task (e.g., teach the element to a younger student)
- Portfolio entry showcasing best work

**Key principle:** Summative assessment is HIGHER STAKES but should still allow multiple demonstrations of mastery (not one-shot exams).

## Differentiation Through ECD

### How ECD Supports All Learners

**For struggling students:**
- Break claims into smaller sub-claims
- Provide more scaffolded tasks
- Accept evidence in multiple formats (draw instead of write, explain orally instead of in writing)
- Allow more time for task completion

**Example:** 
Original claim: "Student understands connection between circular geometry and Shamash mythology"

Differentiated sub-claims:
1. Student knows Shamash was sun god
2. Student knows sun is circular
3. Student can explain that circles represent Shamash

Each sub-claim has simpler evidence requirements.

---

**For advanced students:**
- Add complexity to claims (deeper understanding expectations)
- Design more challenging tasks
- Require evidence of transfer to new contexts
- Encourage original creation/innovation

**Example:**
Advanced claim: "Student can analyze how different civilizations used circular symbolism and hypothesize why variations exist"

Advanced task: "Compare Mesopotamian sun disk, Egyptian solar barque, and Maya calendar circles. What's universal? What's culturally specific? Propose a theory explaining the differences based on environmental or social factors."

## Self-Assessment and Metacognition

### Student-Facing Success Criteria

**Transform claims into "I can" statements:**

From educator claim:
"Student understands that geometric forms carry both mathematical and cultural meaning"

To student-facing criteria:
"I can explain what makes a circle mathematically special (all points equal distance from center) AND what circles meant to Mesopotamians (Shamash, sun, justice, divine order)"

**Student self-check:**
- ☐ I can define a circle mathematically
- ☐ I can identify Shamash and explain his connection to circles
- ☐ I can explain at least 2 reasons why Mesopotamians thought circles were important
- ☐ I can create my own circular design that shows I understand both the math and the meaning

---

### Reflection Prompts

**After each week/element:**

1. **What I learned:** What's the most interesting thing you discovered about [geometric element]?

2. **What challenged me:** What was hardest to understand? What are you still wondering about?

3. **How I used it:** Describe one time you used geometric thinking to solve a problem or create something this week

4. **Connections I made:** How does this element connect to other shapes we've studied? How does it connect to things outside of school?

5. **Next steps:** What do you want to learn more about related to this shape?

## Assessment Data Use

### What Teachers Do With Evidence

**During instruction (formative):**
1. **Identify patterns:** Are most students struggling with the same concept?
2. **Adjust pacing:** Do we need more time on this element?
3. **Differentiate:** Which students need support? Which need challenge?
4. **Provide feedback:** Specific, actionable guidance for improvement

**After instruction (summative):**
1. **Evaluate mastery:** Did students meet the claims?
2. **Assign grades/levels:** What performance level did each student achieve?
3. **Inform future planning:** Which elements need stronger instruction next year?
4. **Communicate progress:** What do we tell families about student learning?

### What Students Do With Evidence

**Self-monitoring:**
1. Track progress on "I can" checklists
2. Identify own areas of strength and challenge
3. Set personal learning goals
4. Request specific help when needed

**Portfolio building:**
1. Select best work demonstrating mastery
2. Reflect on growth over time
3. Showcase learning to families
4. Prepare evidence for next grade level teachers

## Connection to MAGIC Framework

### How ECD Reveals M·A·I·C Understanding

**M (Math) evidence:**
- Accurate calculations
- Correct geometric constructions
- Precise use of mathematical vocabulary
- Logical problem-solving steps

**A (Aesthetic) evidence:**
- Recognizes cultural pattern preferences
- Creates designs reflecting civilization's style
- Articulates artistic choices
- Shows attention to visual composition

**I (Institutional) evidence:**
- Explains mythological/religious significance
- Describes ritual or ceremonial uses
- Connects geometry to belief systems
- Understands what knowledge was valued

**C (Control) evidence:**
- Identifies who had access to geometric knowledge
- Describes power structures around innovation
- Recognizes inequalities in education/opportunity
- Questions who benefited from inventions

**Integrated evidence:**
Tasks requiring convergence of all drivers - "Design a temple that shows mathematical precision, cultural aesthetics, religious meaning, and serves a power function (legitimizing authority)"

## Special Considerations for Project Euclid

### Trauma-Informed Assessment Practices

Given the curriculum's emphasis on accessibility and support for diverse learners:

**Flexibility in demonstration:**
- Allow students to show mastery in ways that feel safe
- Respect energy levels and capacity on given days
- Provide options (write, draw, speak, build)
- No surprise assessments - students always know what's expected

**Emphasis on growth:**
- Compare student to their own previous performance, not to others
- Celebrate small victories and incremental progress
- Frame "mistakes" as learning opportunities
- Provide multiple chances to demonstrate mastery

**Safe environment:**
- Assessment should feel supportive, not punitive
- Reduce performance anxiety through low-stakes formative assessment
- Build confidence before summative evaluation
- Honor different pacing needs

## ECD Implementation Checklist

### For Each Geometric Element

Before teaching:
- ☐ Claims clearly defined (knowledge, skill, understanding)
- ☐ Evidence types identified for each claim
- ☐ Tasks designed with authentic context
- ☐ Rubrics created showing performance levels
- ☐ Differentiation planned (scaffolds and extensions)
- ☐ Student-facing success criteria written

During teaching:
- ☐ Formative assessment embedded daily
- ☐ Student work collected as evidence
- ☐ Feedback provided on progress
- ☐ Instruction adjusted based on data

After teaching:
- ☐ Summative assessment administered
- ☐ Student performance evaluated against claims
- ☐ Levels/grades assigned
- ☐ Reflection on instruction effectiveness
- ☐ Plans for improvement next iteration

## Assessment Examples Bank

### Sample Tasks by Grade Level

**Grade 3 (Circles):**
- Draw and label 5 circles in the classroom
- Create a Shamash sun disk with at least 8 rays
- Measure the diameter of 3 circular objects and calculate circumference (π ≈ 3)

**Grade 4 (8-Pointed Stars):**
- Construct perfect 8-pointed star using rotation method
- Compare Ishtar's star to modern Star of David - similarities and differences?
- Design a tessellation pattern for temple walls

**Grade 5 (Triangles):**
- Build strongest possible structure from toothpicks and clay (discover triangle stability)
- Calculate area of triangular temple pediment
- Compare Egyptian pyramid triangles to Mayan pyramid triangles - why different angles?
